# REVIEW 2 — Assessment Quality & Hiring Signal

Reviewer: Reviewer 2 (assessment validity / hiring signal). Scope: `ASSESSMENT_DESIGN.md`, `SCORING.md`, `DATA_MODEL.md`. Not a redesign — a critical read of whether this instrument will actually rank hundreds of candidates by "smart, capable, independent" better than a CV would.

Findings are prioritized CRITICAL (would likely produce a materially wrong or noisy hiring signal) → IMPORTANT (real validity/fairness risk, should be fixed before or shortly after launch) → MINOR (worth a look, low stakes).

---

## CRITICAL

**1. The highest-weighted pillar (Independence, 30%) rests on the fewest data points (3 items, 9 sub-answers).**
Reasoning gets 6 items, tech 7, speed 10 — independence, the pillar the hiring manager says matters most, gets 3. Each item collapses to a single 0.5/0.25/0.25 composite, so the effective granularity is closer to "3 judgments" than "3 items." With heterogeneous scenario content (webhook vs. SSO vs. cron vs. billing) and only 3 draws from an 8-scenario pool, item-to-item variance will swamp true-ability variance for any two candidates who aren't at the extremes. The pillar most relied on to find "the small number who are genuinely independent" is also the one least able to reliably separate the 90th from the 98th percentile — exactly the comparison the hiring manager needs made correctly.
*Fix:* Either raise investigation item count (even 4–5 shorter scenes), or explicitly report/display a wider confidence band on Independence specifically, or don't let it carry the single highest weight. At minimum, the pilot (`TEST_STRATEGY.md` §9) should compute split-half/item reliability for this pillar, not just timing.

**2. Independence's "process" score (30% of I) is trivially gameable by mechanical tab-clicking, not genuine investigative judgment.**
`I_process` rewards: decisive artifact opened before submit (0.40), ≥3 distinct artifacts opened (0.20), first artifact opened before first answer (0.20), item submitted (0.20). With only 5–6 tabs and a 210s (or 180s, see #6) budget, the dominant strategy for any candidate — independent-minded or not — is "click every tab once, then answer," which maxes out 3 of the 4 process components regardless of whether the candidate reasoned about *which* artifacts mattered. This directly undermines the stated purpose ("observing how they worked... whether they used the whole space") — it measures "did you click around" not "did you know where to look." A genuinely more independent candidate who *correctly infers* which 2 of 6 tabs matter and skips the rest (efficient, evidence-based) is scored *worse* on process than one who opens all 6 out of habit.
*Fix:* Reward efficiency (fewer opens reaching the decisive artifact) rather than exhaustive opening, or drop the "≥3 artifacts" component entirely and weight correctness higher.

**3. The "ask the manager" trap only discriminates once; after item 1 of 3 it stops measuring anything.**
The design deliberately makes "ask the manager what to do" always wrong on the next-action sub-question, calling this "the point." But a test-taker only needs to notice this pattern on their *first* investigation item to mechanically eliminate that option on items 2 and 3 without any independent judgment at all. What was meant as a signal of reflexive escalation becomes, after one exposure, a free elimination heuristic — for every candidate, not just independent ones. This shrinks the option space from 4 to 3 for two-thirds of the block's next-action sub-questions, inflating everyone's apparent score roughly equally and reducing between-candidate discrimination on the exact sub-question meant to isolate independence.
*Fix:* Vary whether escalation is ever correct (rarely, but sometimes — e.g., a scenario where the evidence is genuinely ambiguous or the cheap reversible action doesn't exist), or use different anti-patterns per scenario rather than the same one three times.

**4. Item-family/scenario exposure over a real hiring round is not addressed — only literal-content collision is.**
The design's uniqueness argument ("probability two candidates share an identical item is <1%") is about surface parameters, not about the *trick*. The investigation block has only 8 scenarios × 2–3 cause variants — 16–24 distinct "stories," full stop. Israeli CS students at a handful of programs (this is explicitly the target population) talk to each other on WhatsApp/Discord/course group chats. After the first few dozen candidates in a "hundreds of applicants" round, it is highly likely that "it's always one of: webhook secret rotated / endpoint moved / API key expired" and "never pick the ask-the-manager option" become common knowledge, well before the round finishes. The same applies at smaller scale to the 18 named primitives behind `reasoning.rule_induction` and the 14 tech scenario "morals" (429 → respect Retry-After; env diff → check the port; SPF/DKIM → email deliverability). None of this requires copying an actual item — it requires one classmate finishing the assessment and describing the *shape* of what they saw, which the anti-copy/SVG/right-click defenses do nothing to prevent.
*Fix:* Track this explicitly as a residual risk (it's currently invisible in the docs), consider a larger scenario library before scaling past a few hundred candidates, and consider randomizing which anti-pattern/trap appears per scenario instance so "the trick" isn't stable across sittings.

**5. Tech aptitude items frequently test specific applied/ops knowledge, not "instinct," contradicting the design's own stated goal.**
Several worked items require recall of a convention rather than reasoning from first principles: HTTP status semantics and `Retry-After` (ex. 8), SPF/DKIM for email deliverability, RBAC/least-privilege framing, cron-timezone gotchas. A candidate who has done a web/DevOps internship will recognize these instantly; an equally sharp CS student who has only done algorithms/theory coursework will effectively be guessing among plausible-sounding options — not because they lack "instinct" but because they've never seen a `429` in the wild. This risks the tech pillar quietly re-measuring "prior industry exposure" (which correlates with SES, connections, and who already has a CV worth noticing) rather than raw technical aptitude — the opposite of the stated goal of the whole platform (find signal *beyond* the CV).
*Fix:* For each tech template, ask "could a strong CS student with zero industry exposure derive this from the artifact alone, or does it require having seen this exact convention before?" Templates like `env_diff_bug` and `sql_outcome` pass this test (derivable from the artifact); `http_status_next`, `email_undelivered`'s SPF/DKIM, and `least_privilege` are closer to recall. Consider giving the relevant convention *in* the artifact (as `speed.timezone_shift` already does by supplying the offset) so the item tests reasoning-with-a-fact rather than recall-of-a-fact.

**6. Live inconsistency between design docs on investigation time limit (210s vs. 180s) — and it matters because the margin is already thin.**
`ASSESSMENT_DESIGN.md` §2/§2.2 fixes the investigation time limit at 210s, with its own rationale table stating a strong candidate needs 120–180s. `DATA_MODEL.md` §3.3's example blueprint JSON instead specifies `"time_limit_s":180` for the `investigate` block. Both documents are marked "Decided." If 180s is what actually ships (the blueprint JSON is literally what the zod-validated config loads), the flagship independence pillar would run with *zero* margin for a "strong candidate" by the design's own numbers, directly threatening the "does timing cut off genuinely strong candidates" concern this review was asked to probe.
*Fix:* Reconcile before implementation; whichever number is correct, restate it consistently in both documents and confirm which one the seed migration (`0002_seed.sql`) actually uses.

**7. The investigation block's own scoring incentivizes guessing over honest skipping — contradicting the design's stated principle.**
`ASSESSMENT_DESIGN.md` §2.3 states skipping is "the honest alternative to guessing" and is "never penalized beyond scoring 0." But inside the investigation block specifically, `I_process` gives 0.20 of the process score for "item submitted (not expired)" — meaning an expired/skipped item loses not just the 0.5/0.25/0.25 correctness credit (same as a wrong guess would) but *also* 20% of the process score, while a non-fast wrong guess loses nothing extra (the guess-penalty only triggers on fast wrong answers, <25% of the time limit). A rational candidate who genuinely doesn't know the root cause is therefore better off submitting *any* answer slowly (even a coin-flip taken near the deadline) than honestly skipping — the opposite of the incentive the design intends, and precisely in the block meant to measure honest, non-bluffing behavior. The "skip vs. guess discipline" signal described in §3.5 is only computed from the *speed* block, not applied here where it matters most.
*Fix:* Either drop the "submitted" component from `I_process`, or make guess-penalty apply regardless of response time within the investigation block, or extend the skip-vs-guess discipline signal to investigation items.

## IMPORTANT

**8. Strict per-item timing is a real, only partially-acknowledged tradeoff against slow-but-excellent thinkers.**
The design is explicit that time limits are set to deny the "copy → LLM → answer" loop, and separately targets "≥70% of strong students finish with ≥15% time left" in a pre-launch pilot. That's an *aggregate* target on a small, self-selected pilot group ("recruited from personal networks," per `OPEN_QUESTIONS.md` #3) — it does not protect the specific genuinely brilliant-but-deliberate candidate who is the false negative this design risks creating. There is no post-launch recalibration (`SCORING.md` §9: "no automatic recalibration"), so if the real applicant pool's tail differs from the pilot's, this drifts silently and is only caught if a human happens to look at the bank-analytics page.
*Fix:* Acknowledge this tradeoff explicitly as accepted risk (it may well be the right call given the anti-LLM constraint), but add an ongoing check — e.g., flag in bank analytics when a template's expiry rate for otherwise-high-scoring candidates is unusually high, which would indicate the timer, not ability, is the binding constraint for your best candidates.

**9. The Speed pillar's "pace" component leaks into every other pillar, double-penalizing careful reasoners.**
`S_pace` is computed from `response_ms` on *every* item across *all* blocks with `s_i ≥ 0.75`, not just the 10 dedicated speed-block items. A candidate who reasons carefully and correctly but slowly on the reasoning/tech/investigation items already risks running short on those blocks' own time limits (finding #8) — and is *then* additionally marked down on the nominally separate Speed pillar (15% weight) for the same slow-but-correct behavior. This blurs the four pillars' claimed independence (R and T don't use time, but S effectively re-scores how fast R/T/I were answered) and means "careful and correct" is penalized twice by different routes, while contributing to Overall through both the direct pillar weight and this cross-block channel.
*Fix:* Compute `S_pace` only from the dedicated speed-block items (which is presumably what "pace" is meant to capture), or explicitly document that Speed is intentionally a whole-assessment tempo measure and reconsider whether it should carry 15% independently of the pillars whose timing it's re-deriving from.

**10. Hebrew-fluency-driven timing bias is plausible and untested against the real applicant pool.**
Investigation tickets and chat artifacts are written in natural, colloquial Hebrew prose (e.g., the Slack excerpt in worked example 6) under a strict 210s/180s clock for the whole multi-artifact scene. Reading speed in Hebrew is not uniform across the pool this platform will actually see (new immigrants/olim in CS programs, Arab-Israeli CS students for whom Hebrew is a second language, ultra-Orthodox students from different literacy tracks) — none of this is reasoning ability, all of it consumes the same fixed clock. The pilot group is explicitly "recruited from personal networks" (`OPEN_QUESTIONS.md` #3), which is likely to be linguistically homogeneous and will not surface this.
*Fix:* At minimum, track completion/expiry rates by self-reported or inferred demographic proxies (institution, degree program) post-launch and look for systematic gaps; consider a modest flat time buffer on investigation items given they are prose-heavy relative to the other three blocks.

**11. Several "speed" items are factual-knowledge checks wearing a speed costume, not pure fast-execution/reasoning, and reward specific coursework exposure.**
`speed.path_resolve` (Unix relative-path semantics), `speed.base_small` (binary↔decimal), `speed.regex_match`, and `speed.ip_valid`/CIDR validity all require having previously learned a specific convention, not deriving one on the spot — closer to the "no trivia" line the design says it wants to avoid than the design acknowledges. Because these facts map onto specific courses (networks, systems, discrete math), they will systematically favor later-year students over first/second-year students independent of underlying reasoning ability or independence, even though the role explicitly has "growth path" framing that doesn't require seniority.
*Fix:* Keep these (they're legitimately CS-adjacent for the target population) but treat them as intentionally testing "CS fluency," not "processing speed" or "reasoning," when interpreting the Speed pillar; consider tagging them separately in bank analytics so a future review can see if they're driving most of the Speed variance.

**12. Named/closed "trick" libraries behind combinatorially large item counts are coachable.**
`reasoning.rule_induction` draws from "a library of 18 primitives" and the tech/investigation blocks draw from closed lists of root-cause archetypes and anti-patterns. The >500k / >8k variant counts quoted throughout the doc describe surface parameter space, not concept space — the actual number of *distinct insights* a candidate needs to have ready is much smaller (18 primitives; ~8 investigation "morals"; a dozen tech "gotchas"). A candidate (or a prep group, per finding #4) who has enumerated these in advance has a real, coachable advantage that has nothing to do with live reasoning or independence.
*Fix:* No action needed for launch, but don't cite the raw variant counts as evidence of resistance to preparation — they aren't the same claim as resistance to content collision, which is the only claim they actually support.

## MINOR

**13. Difficulty weights (1.0/1.3/1.7) are asserted by design, not empirically validated per generated instance.**
The CI bank audit (`ASSESSMENT_DESIGN.md` §4.4) checks that the *count* of items per difficulty matches the blueprint mix and that scoring logic is internally consistent, but nothing verifies that a given "difficulty 3" instance is actually harder than a "difficulty 2" one for procedurally generated content (e.g., some `rule_induction` difficulty-3 compositions may accidentally be easy to spot). Since difficulty weights directly scale into R and T, mislabeled difficulty adds unweighted noise exactly where the design intends extra signal.
*Fix:* Use the pilot and post-launch bank analytics (which already track per-template accuracy) to spot-check that difficulty-3 instances empirically score lower than difficulty-1/2 instances of the same family; flag families that don't.

**14. The Speed accuracy gate is a hard step function at a single threshold (60%).**
A candidate at 59% vs. 61% overall accuracy sees `S_raw` capped at 0.50 vs. uncapped — a potentially large, discontinuous swing in the Speed score (and hence Overall) for a one-item difference near a boundary that has no special significance. This adds ranking noise right around a plausible pass/fail line.
*Fix:* Consider a smoothed penalty (e.g., linear taper below 65%) rather than a hard cliff at 60%.

**15. The flagship worked investigation example is on the easy side for separating strong from exceptional candidates.**
Worked example 6 (`investigate.webhook_missing`) hands the candidate three converging, unambiguous clues in adjacent artifacts: a 401/`sig=invalid` log cluster starting exactly at the secret-rotation timestamp, and a Slack message from the person who made the change ("דנה: סידרתי את ההגדרות של החנות אתמול בערב") sitting right next to it. Any candidate who opens 2 of the 6 tabs and reads carefully will get it — there's no genuinely conflicting evidence to weigh, just one decoy (the Node upgrade note) that a moment's date-comparison rules out. If this worked example is representative of the bank's actual difficulty, the investigation block may function more as a "did you read the artifacts at all" floor test than a discriminator among already-competent candidates — which matters because this is exactly the pillar meant to isolate the top few.
*Fix:* If other scenarios/variants in the bank are meaningfully harder (genuinely conflicting evidence, no single artifact that "gives it away"), say so explicitly in the doc; if this example is typical, raise the ambiguity/decoy strength for at least some variants so the block has headroom at the top of the distribution.

---

## Overall verdict

This design is well-engineered against the threat it was explicitly built to defeat — real-time LLM externalization — and the four-pillar framing, server-authoritative timing, and deterministic parameterized generation are all sound engineering choices that will comfortably beat "read the CV" as a first-pass filter for *obviously* weak or fabricated applicants. But as an instrument for the harder job actually being asked of it — reliably ranking hundreds of already-selected CS students to find the handful who are exceptionally independent and technically sharp — it has real validity gaps: the most heavily weighted pillar (independence) has the least data and a process-score that rewards clicking over judgment; several tech and speed items measure prior exposure to specific technical conventions rather than instinct or fluid reasoning; the anti-pattern traps and closed scenario/primitive libraries will very plausibly become common knowledge among a socially connected candidate population well before a "hundreds of applicants" round concludes; and the strict timing, while a defensible tradeoff against LLM use, is uncalibrated against the actual applicant distribution and creates a secondary bias against careful, non-native-Hebrew, or less-experienced-but-sharp candidates. None of this is fatal, and most of it is fixable without touching the anti-cheat architecture — but as specified, the resulting scores will meaningfully outperform a CV for filtering out the bottom of the pool, while carrying enough noise and exposure risk that the top of the ranked list should still be read as "worth a closer human look," not as a validated measurement of who the best candidates actually are.
